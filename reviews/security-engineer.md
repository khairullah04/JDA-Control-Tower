**Top 3 risks**

1. **The passkeys do not protect what they claim to.** Portal data is world-writable in Firestore (#1, #8). The passkeys themselves are committed, 4-5 digits long and open to brute force (#2, #7).
2. **Anyone can become a "signed-in user".** Open registration plus a wildcard rule gives any stranger write access to JDAs, budgets and the activity log, and read access to the invoice inbox and legacy invoice collections without the tracker passkey (#3, #4).
3. **Finance data can be changed without a truthful trail.** Stored XSS from a self-registered account lets an attacker act with a passkey holder's session (#5). Un-audited fields and weak actor identity then hide or misattribute the change (#9, #10).

**What's done well**

- CJI3 and Payment Table data never touches the browser's Firestore access. `finance*` collections are closed in the rules, and every write is validated, recomputed and committed together with its audit lines on the server (`jda/server/routes/finance.js`, `jda/web/js/finance/service.js`). Deletes are soft.
- Passkey checking is server-side with a timing-safe compare, exchanged for an HMAC-signed, expiring, scope-bound token that is invalidated when the passkey changes (`jda/server/passkeys.js:19-28`). The tracker's token cannot open a JDA and the reverse is also true.
- Output handling is mostly careful. `esc()` is used consistently, `cleanHtml` is an allowlist sanitiser applied on save and again on display (`portal/js/blocks.js:50-81`), links are restricted to http(s), and AI output is re-shaped field by field and can only create Pending drafts.

**Score:** 3/10 — the finance server core is soundly designed, but committed trivial passkeys, open registration, a world-writable portal database and a stored XSS mean the controls around it can all be bypassed today.
